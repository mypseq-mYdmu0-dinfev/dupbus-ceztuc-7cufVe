## 1. Introduction

In knowledge‑based firms (KBFs), including legal, accounting, consulting, IT, and R&D organisations, individual expertise constitutes the primary source of competitive advantage (Grant, 1996). Unlike traditional manufacturers relying primarily on physical assets, KBFs derive strategic value from the intellectual capital, professional judgement, and collaborative knowledge exchange of people. This structural dependence on individual excellence creates an inherent paradox: the very environments that require outstanding performance can generate significant social friction when that performance becomes conspicuous.

Tall Poppy Syndrome (TPS), the social phenomenon whereby high-performing individuals face peer marginalisation in response to their standout performance (Feather, 1989), represents precisely this paradox. In KBFs, where collaboration and knowledge sharing are operational necessities rather than cultural preferences, TPS-driven dynamics carry compounding organisational consequences. When marginalised, a high-performing individual (Tall Poppy/TP) may possibly respond by prioritising individual output over collective knowledge contribution, creating latent knowledge management (KM) governance risks that convert interpersonal friction into a board-level strategic concern. Concurrently, team cohesion erodes as collaborative norms break down under performance disparity tensions that management rarely observes from above.

Despite extensive cultural and social psychology scholarship on TPS (Feather, 1989, 1994), its application as a management challenge within KBF contexts remains largely a literature gap. This research explores TPS dynamics across multiple KBFs, examining from the perspective of Tall Poppies’ Peers (TPPs), colleagues who directly observed TPS-related team dynamics. Because TPS is a supra-organisational phenomenon, driven by human psychology and group dynamics that transcend individual firm boundaries, a cross-firm study design is both methodologically appropriate and empirically necessary: confining to a single organisation risks conflating findings with that firm's idiosyncratic leadership culture, obscuring the phenomenon itself.

The research aim is to investigate how TPS dynamics in KBFs generate team cohesion and knowledge governance challenges, exposing management implications for practitioners. Four research objectives guide this project:
- **RO1:** Critically review existing literature on TPS, team dynamics, and KM in KBFs
- **RO2:** Explore how TPPs in KBFs perceive and experience TPS dynamics
- **RO3:** Evaluate the organisational and interpersonal conditions that exacerbate or alleviate TPS
- **RO4:** Develop management recommendations addressing TPS-related KM and team cohesion risks

Two research questions frame the investigation:
- **RQ1:** What conditions within KBFs exacerbate or alleviate TPS?
- **RQ2:** How do team members in KBFs experience TPS-related team dynamics?

This study contributes **academically** by extending TPS studies beyond cultural psychology into management and KM domains, and **practically** by equipping managers with workforce-derived insights to address an under-recognised governance challenge.

## 2. Literature Review

**2.1. Tall Poppy Syndrome: Origins and Professional Manifestation**

TPS was formally theorised by Feather (1989, 1994), who identified a culturally rooted disposition towards marginalising high achievers whose success appears disproportionate to perceived deservingness. Originally examined within Australian and New Zealand cultural contexts, TPS describes the peer-level attribution of resentment, hostility, and social exclusion directed at standout performers. Subsequent organisational literature extended these foundations into workplace settings, identifying TPS as a driver of reduced collaboration, peer disengagement, and exclusion of TP from informal group networks. In professional servicing environments, where individual intellectual performance constitutes the organisation’s core commercial offering, the structural conditions for TPS are amplified: excellence is simultaneously required and inherently comparative.

**2.2. Knowledge-Based Firms and the Strategic Stakes of Knowledge Management**

The KBF construct draws from the Knowledge-Based View (KBV) of the firm, advanced by Grant (1996) and Kogut and Zander (1992), which positions knowledge as the primary source of sustained competitive advantage. In KBFs, value creation occurs through expertise application, professional collaboration, and the voluntary sharing of tacit and explicit knowledge, rendering KM not merely an operational function but a strategic imperative. Where voluntary knowledge sharing breaks down, competitive capability is directly undermined. This framing elevates TPS from a team relations matter to a KM governance concern: TPS dynamics induce TPs to withdraw from collective knowledge exchange; and the organisation's intellectual capital base is eroded at its most strategically significant node.

**2.3. Team Dynamics and Peer Reactions to High Performance**

Social Comparison Theory (Festinger, 1954) offers a foundational account of peer responses to outstanding performance. In contexts of upward social comparison, where peers perceive a TP's performance as threatening to their own relative standing, hostile attribution and social exclusion behaviours may emerge as self-protective mechanisms. Organisational behaviour research extending this framework demonstrates that performance disparity within teams is associated with reduced collaborative behaviour, peer withdrawal, and cohesion erosion. Organisational Citizenship Behaviour literature further indicates that voluntary knowledge sharing is contingent on perceived relational equity and group belonging, conditions directly threatened by TPS-driven marginalisation.

**2.4. Knowledge-Sharing Behaviour and Conditions for Withdrawal**

Social Exchange Theory (Blau, 1964) frames knowledge sharing as a reciprocal exchange: individuals contribute when they perceive equivalent value returned. Whilst TPS dynamics disrupt perceived reciprocity through social exclusion, devaluation of contribution, or peer hostility, TPs may recalibrate knowledge contribution downward. The KM literature on sharing inhibition identifies relational trust, psychological safety, and collaborative culture as necessary preconditions for sustained voluntary sharing (Cabrera and Cabrera, 2002). These conditions are precisely those threatened by TPS dynamics. Critically, this study does not assume knowledge withdrawal as a confirmed outcome; rather, it proceeds from the position that TPS may reshape knowledge-sharing behaviour in ways that generate latent KM risks. This constitutes the central empirical inquiry.

**2.5. Literature Gap and Conceptual Framework**

Existing TPS scholarship is concentrated in cultural psychology and attitudinal research; management-oriented applications within KBF contexts are rarely found. Research on high performer team dynamics tends to assume net-positive organisational outcomes for individual excellence, overlooking peer-level and KM costs. This study occupies the intersection of these gaps, framing TPS as a KM governance and team cohesion challenge specific to the KBF context.

The provisional conceptual framework synthesises four thematic domains: (1) TPS conditions within KBF environments; (2) peer team experience of TPS dynamics; (3) KM sharing behaviour; and (4) team cohesion outcomes. These domains are connected through relational linkages rather than directional causation, consistent with interpretivist epistemology. The framework will be updated post-analysis to reflect emergent empirical patterns.

## 3. Research Methodology

**3.1. Research Philosophy and Approach**

This study adopts an interpretivist philosophy, grounded in a constructivist ontology and subjectivist epistemology. TPS dynamics, characterised by perception, social attribution, relational interpretation, and lived professional experience, cannot be meaningfully reduced to objective measurement alone. An inductive, qualitative approach is therefore most appropriate, enabling investigation of peer experience within team environments without imposing predetermined hypotheses on a phenomenon that is inherently socially constructed and context-dependent.

**3.2. Data Collection: Semi-Structured Interviews**

Semi-structured interviews constitute the primary data collection method. This approach affords the flexibility to probe emergent themes whilst maintaining sufficient structure for cross-participant comparability. Given that TPS dynamics are embedded in interpersonal and contextual experience, semi-structured interviews allow participants to articulate observations within their own professional frames of reference, a critical advantage when the phenomenon under investigation is socially constructed and contextually variable. Interviews and transcription will be conducted via Teams in accordance with UoL research integrity requirements.

**3.3. Sampling**

At least 6 participants, consistent with qualitative sample size conventions for thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006), will be recruited through purposive sampling, targeting TPPs: non-managerial professionals in KBFs who have worked alongside a standout performer and observed team-level friction or dynamic shifts in that context. The eligibility criterion asks whether participants have worked alongside someone their team recognised as a standout performer, and whether they observed tension or changes in team dynamics in that context.

Sampling will follow a cross-organisational design. TPS is a supra-organisational phenomenon, driven by human psychology and group dynamics that transcend individual firm boundaries; studying it within a single organisation risks conflating findings with that firm's particular culture, making it impossible to isolate TPS as the explanatory variable. Cross-firm sampling isolates the phenomenon and elicits findings with greater transferability across KBFs.

The peer-primary participant design is deliberate rather than a constraint of access. TPS dynamics are most visible and experienced laterally within teams; peer perspectives reveal behavioural patterns not fully observable from management vantage points, where hierarchical distance limits disclosure. TPPs are therefore proximal experiencers of the phenomenon, and their accounts constitute the most direct empirical window into the research questions.

**3.4. Data Analysis**

Thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase framework will be applied. Coding will commence after the third interview and continue iteratively until theoretical saturation is reached. A dedicated coding node for disconfirming evidence, including instances of continued or increased knowledge sharing despite TPS conditions, will be maintained throughout analysis, ensuring analytical balance and guarding against asymmetric interpretation and confirmation bias.

**3.5. Trustworthiness**

Qualitative rigour will be addressed across four criteria (Lincoln and Guba, 1985). Credibility will be maintained through systematic coding and, where achievable, member checking. Dependability will be supported through a maintained audit trail of analytical decisions. Transferability is addressed through cross-firm sampling and rich contextual description. Confirmability is addressed through researcher reflexivity: the researcher's professional background, including sustained exposure to high-performer team environments, is acknowledged as an interpretive lens and disclosed in this section.

**3.6. Feasibility and Limitations**

The researcher's (Culous Yu) professional network spans legal, accounting, consulting, technology, entertainment, and public sector organisations across more than 10 countries, providing realistic access to a diverse KBF sample. The primary limitation is the small qualitative sample, which restricts statistical generalisation. This is an accepted constraint within interpretivist research design, where analytical generalisation to theory, rather than to populations, constitutes the relevant standard (Lincoln and Guba, 1985).

**3.7. Ethics**

Standard University of Liverpool ethics approval will be sought prior to any data collection. Interview questions will operate at the professional observation level, examining team behaviour and collaboration patterns rather than personal emotional disclosure. Individual participant consent forms will be signed by all participants in advance, and all data stored exclusively on OneDrive. Data collection will not commence until written ethical approval has been received.

## S03:S3: Research Methodology

**Research Philosophy and Approach**

This study adopts an interpretivist philosophy, grounded in a constructivist ontology and subjectivist epistemology. TPS dynamics, characterised by perception, social attribution, relational interpretation, and lived professional experience, cannot be meaningfully reduced to objective measurement alone. An inductive, qualitative approach is therefore most appropriate, enabling investigation of peer experience within team environments without imposing predetermined hypotheses on a phenomenon that is inherently socially constructed and context-dependent.

**Data Collection: Semi-Structured Interviews**

Semi-structured interviews constitute the primary data collection method. This approach affords the flexibility to probe emergent themes whilst maintaining sufficient structure for cross-participant comparability. Given that TPS dynamics are embedded in interpersonal and contextual experience, semi-structured interviews allow respondents to articulate observations within their own professional frames of reference, a critical advantage when the phenomenon under investigation is socially constructed and contextually variable. All interviews will be conducted via Microsoft Teams; transcription will be performed using Microsoft Teams, Microsoft Office, or Microsoft Copilot exclusively, in accordance with UoL research integrity requirements.

**Sampling**

Participants will be recruited through purposive sampling, targeting TPPs: non-managerial professionals in KBFs who have worked alongside a standout performer and observed team-level friction or dynamic shifts in that context. The eligibility criterion asks whether participants have worked alongside someone their team recognised as a standout performer, and whether they observed tension or changes in team dynamics in that context. A minimum of six participants will be recruited, consistent with qualitative sample size conventions for thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006).

Sampling will follow a cross-organisational design, with one participant recruited per organisation. This design is both methodologically and ethically motivated. TPS is a supra-organisational phenomenon, driven by human psychology and group dynamics that transcend individual firm boundaries; studying it within a single organisation risks conflating findings with that firm's particular leadership culture, making it impossible to isolate TPS as the explanatory variable. Cross-firm sampling isolates the phenomenon and produces findings with greater transferability across the KBF sector. Additionally, recruiting one participant per organisation removes the requirement for organisational consent letters, as individual participant consent alone is sufficient.

The peer-primary participant design is deliberate rather than a constraint of access. TPS dynamics are most visible and experienced laterally within teams; peer perspectives reveal behavioural patterns not fully observable from management vantage points, where hierarchical distance limits disclosure. TPPs are therefore proximal experiencers of the phenomenon, and their accounts constitute the most direct empirical window into the research questions.

**Data Analysis**

Thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase framework will be applied. Coding will commence after the second or third interview and continue iteratively until theoretical saturation is reached. A dedicated coding node for disconfirming evidence, including instances of continued or increased knowledge sharing despite TPS conditions, will be maintained throughout analysis, ensuring analytical balance and guarding against asymmetric interpretation.

**Trustworthiness**

Qualitative rigour will be addressed across four criteria (Lincoln and Guba, 1985). Credibility will be maintained through systematic coding and, where achievable, member checking. Dependability will be supported through a maintained audit trail of analytical decisions. Transferability is addressed through cross-firm sampling and rich contextual description. Confirmability is addressed through researcher reflexivity: the researcher's professional background, including sustained exposure to high-performer team environments, is acknowledged as an interpretive lens and disclosed in this section.

**Feasibility and Limitations**

The researcher's professional network spans consulting, technology, entertainment, hospitality, and public sector organisations across more than ten countries, providing realistic access to a geographically diverse KBF sample. The one-per-organisation design removes institutional consent barriers and supports recruitment at scale. The primary limitation is the small qualitative sample, which restricts statistical generalisation. This is an accepted constraint within interpretivist research design, where analytical generalisation to theory, rather than to populations, constitutes the relevant standard (Lincoln and Guba, 1985).

**Ethics**

Standard University of Liverpool ethics approval will be sought prior to any data collection. Interview questions will operate at the professional observation level, examining team behaviour and collaboration patterns rather than personal emotional disclosure. Individual participant consent forms will be obtained from all participants, and all data stored exclusively on University of Liverpool OneDrive. Data collection will not commence until written ethical approval has been received.

Knowledge-Based Firms and the Strategic Stakes of Knowledge Management

The KBF construct draws from the Knowledge-Based View (KBV) of the firm, advanced by Grant (1996) and Kogut and Zander (1992), which positions knowledge as the primary source of sustained competitive advantage. In KBFs, value creation occurs through expertise application, professional collaboration, and the voluntary sharing of tacit and explicit knowledge, rendering KM not merely an operational function but a strategic imperative. Where voluntary knowledge sharing breaks down, competitive capability is directly undermined. This framing elevates TPS from a team relations matter to a KM governance concern: where TPS dynamics induce high performers to withdraw from collective knowledge exchange, the organisation's intellectual capital base is eroded at its most strategically significant node.

Team Dynamics and Peer Reactions to High Performance

Social Comparison Theory (Festinger, 1954) offers a foundational account of peer responses to outstanding performance. In contexts of upward social comparison, where peers perceive a Tall Poppy's performance as threatening to their own relative standing, hostile attribution and social exclusion behaviours may emerge as self-protective mechanisms. Organisational behaviour research extending this framework demonstrates that performance disparity within teams is associated with reduced collaborative behaviour, peer withdrawal, and cohesion erosion. Organisational Citizenship Behaviour (OCB) literature further indicates that voluntary knowledge sharing is contingent on perceived relational equity and group belonging, conditions directly threatened by TPS-driven marginalisation.

Knowledge-Sharing Behaviour and Conditions for Withdrawal

Social Exchange Theory (Blau, 1964) frames knowledge sharing as a reciprocal exchange: individuals contribute when they perceive equivalent value returned. Where TPS dynamics disrupt perceived reciprocity through social exclusion, devaluation of contribution, or peer hostility, Tall Poppies may recalibrate knowledge contribution downward. The KM literature on sharing inhibition identifies relational trust, psychological safety, and collaborative culture as necessary preconditions for sustained voluntary sharing (Cabrera and Cabrera, 2002). These conditions are precisely those threatened by TPS dynamics. Critically, this study does not assume knowledge withdrawal as a confirmed outcome; rather, it proceeds from the position that TPS may reshape knowledge-sharing behaviour in ways that generate latent KM risks. This constitutes the central empirical inquiry.

Literature Gap and Conceptual Framework

Existing TPS scholarship is concentrated in cultural psychology and attitudinal research; management-oriented applications within KBF contexts are largely absent. Research on high performer team dynamics tends to assume net-positive organisational outcomes for individual excellence, overlooking peer-level and KM costs. This study occupies the intersection of these gaps, framing TPS as a KM governance and team cohesion challenge specific to the KBF context.

The provisional conceptual framework synthesises four thematic domains: TPS conditions within KBF environments; peer team experience of TP dynamics; KM sharing behaviour; and team cohesion outcomes. These domains are connected through relational linkages rather than directional causation, consistent with interpretivist epistemology. The framework will be updated post-analysis to reflect emergent empirical patterns.

S3: Research Methodology

Research Philosophy and Approach

This study adopts an interpretivist philosophy, grounded in a constructivist ontology and subjectivist epistemology. TPS dynamics, characterised by perception, social attribution, relational interpretation, and lived professional experience, cannot be meaningfully reduced to objective measurement alone. An inductive, qualitative approach is therefore most appropriate, enabling investigation of peer experience within team environments without imposing predetermined hypotheses on a phenomenon that is inherently socially constructed and context-dependent.

Data Collection: Semi-Structured Interviews

Semi-structured interviews constitute the primary data collection method. This approach affords the flexibility to probe emergent themes whilst maintaining sufficient structure for cross-participant comparability. Given that TPS dynamics are embedded in interpersonal and contextual experience, semi-structured interviews allow respondents to articulate observations within their own professional frames of reference, a critical advantage when the phenomenon under investigation is socially constructed and contextually variable. All interviews will be conducted via Microsoft Teams; transcription will be performed using Microsoft Teams, Microsoft Office, or Microsoft Copilot exclusively, in accordance with UoL research integrity requirements.

Sampling

Participants will be recruited through purposive sampling, targeting TPPs: non-managerial professionals in KBFs who have worked alongside a standout performer and observed team-level friction or dynamic shifts in that context. The eligibility criterion asks whether participants have worked alongside someone their team recognised as a standout performer, and whether they observed tension or changes in team dynamics in that context. A minimum of six participants will be recruited, consistent with qualitative sample size conventions for thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006).

Sampling will follow a cross-organisational design, with one participant recruited per organisation. This design is both methodologically and ethically motivated. TPS is a supra-organisational phenomenon, driven by human psychology and group dynamics that transcend individual firm boundaries; studying it within a single organisation risks conflating findings with that firm's particular leadership culture, making it impossible to isolate TPS as the explanatory variable. Cross-firm sampling isolates the phenomenon and produces findings with greater transferability across the KBF sector. Additionally, recruiting one participant per organisation removes the requirement for organisational consent letters, as individual participant consent alone is sufficient.

The peer-primary participant design is deliberate rather than a constraint of access. TPS dynamics are most visible and experienced laterally within teams; peer perspectives reveal behavioural patterns not fully observable from management vantage points, where hierarchical distance limits disclosure. TPPs are therefore proximal experiencers of the phenomenon, and their accounts constitute the most direct empirical window into the research questions.

Data Analysis

Thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase framework will be applied. Coding will commence after the second or third interview and continue iteratively until theoretical saturation is reached. A dedicated coding node for disconfirming evidence, including instances of continued or increased knowledge sharing despite TPS conditions, will be maintained throughout analysis, ensuring analytical balance and guarding against asymmetric interpretation.

Trustworthiness

Qualitative rigour will be addressed across four criteria (Lincoln and Guba, 1985). Credibility will be maintained through systematic coding and, where achievable, member checking. Dependability will be supported through a maintained audit trail of analytical decisions. Transferability is addressed through cross-firm sampling and rich contextual description. Confirmability is addressed through researcher reflexivity: the researcher's professional background, including sustained exposure to high-performer team environments, is acknowledged as an interpretive lens and disclosed in this section.

Feasibility and Limitations

The researcher's professional network spans consulting, technology, entertainment, hospitality, and public sector organisations across more than ten countries, providing realistic access to a geographically diverse KBF sample. The one-per-organisation design removes institutional consent barriers and supports recruitment at scale. The primary limitation is the small qualitative sample, which restricts statistical generalisation. This is an accepted constraint within interpretivist research design, where analytical generalisation to theory, rather than to populations, constitutes the relevant standard (Lincoln and Guba, 1985).

Ethics

Standard University of Liverpool ethics approval will be sought prior to any data collection. Interview questions will operate at the professional observation level, examining team behaviour and collaboration patterns rather than personal emotional disclosure. Individual participant consent forms will be obtained from all participants, and all data stored exclusively on University of Liverpool OneDrive. Data collection will not commence until written ethical approval has been received.
