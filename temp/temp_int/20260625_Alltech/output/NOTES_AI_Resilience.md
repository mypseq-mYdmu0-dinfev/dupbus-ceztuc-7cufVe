Why I think this industry, and Alltech specifically, holds up against AI. Four layers, each one a fallback if the last turns out wrong.

**One. Starting a rival is genuinely hard.**
A marketing agency needs a laptop and a few clients to open its doors. A company that can compete with Alltech needs millions of dollars in LED walls, audio rigs, and staging gear sitting in a warehouse before it quotes a single job. Anyone can register a company tomorrow. Almost nobody can write that cheque tomorrow. That gap is the real moat, and it has nothing to do with how good anyone’s marketing is.

**Two. The work itself resists being standardised.**
Zoom out from marketing to the industry underneath it. Every event is a different room, a different crowd, and a different thing going wrong at two in the afternoon on the day. Current AI is extremely good at pattern repetition; even the most advanced physical robots today are built and trained for controlled, repeatable environments. A live event is close to the opposite of that. It’s one-off, live, and high stakes, and someone has to fix a problem in real time with their own hands and judgement rather than a training set. I don’t think this industry gets automated away soon, not because AI is weak, but because the job resists standardisation by design.

**Three. Not being a necessity is, oddly, protective.**
Nobody needs a launch party, and on the surface that sounds like a weakness. But think about what actually gets automated first. Manufacturing, accounting, legal document review, anything under constant pressure to strip out labour because the output is standardised and the buyer never notices who or what produced it. Nobody pays extra for a human-audited tax return. An event, though, is partly bought because it’s a human production; the value sits in the experience being real, live, and handled by people in the moment. The very thing that makes this industry look fragile, that nobody strictly needs it, is what keeps the automation pressure off it. I would rather be in an industry that people choose but don’t need and that resists standardisation, than one that’s needed but easy to standardise.

**Four. Even the worst case still has an exit.**
If I’m wrong about all of the above and the whole industry does end up automated one day, Alltech still owns the physical kit, the one thing that never disappears. Somebody, human or AI agent, still has to put a screen and a speaker into a room. Whoever owns the screens and speakers gets paid either way. That’s the layer underneath everything else, and it’s why I’d call this a genuinely resilient bet rather than a hopeful one.
