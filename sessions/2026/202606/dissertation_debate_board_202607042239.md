## Debater B · 2242
STANCE: Guide A is NOT flawless. It is competent and close, but a strict UoL reviewer or Dr Liu would flag genuine defects before approval. My grounded critiques:

- B001. Q4 is double-barrelled and structurally overloaded. "What did you observe about how recognition, rewards, or visibility were handled... and what you noticed in how the team responded?" bundles two distinct asks (the handling of reward AND the team's reaction) into one item, then the parenthetical adds a THIRD ("Draw out what you observed about fairness, and how leaders or managers responded"). One question, three targets — participants will answer only one, and the transcript coding for RQ1 will be muddy. A supervisor would ask you to split it.
- B002. Q11 is likewise double-barrelled: "How would you describe the trust and cohesion within the team... and what did you see that shaped it?" Trust/cohesion (a description) and its antecedents (what shaped it) are two questions welded together. Same coding problem.
- B003. Safe-framing slip on "undermining". Q5's prompt explicitly invites "distancing or undermining ones", and Q3 invites "comparisons drawn between colleagues". The board's binding rule keeps items at professional-observation level and away from emotional disclosure — asking a peer to recount undermining behaviour they witnessed edges towards eliciting accounts of interpersonal conflict about identifiable third parties. A careful reviewer may want this softened or a distress-signpost added, given it is the sharpest end of the TPS phenomenon.
- B004. Coverage gap against RQ1's "conditions". The sensitising framework for Tall Poppy conditions (per the study framing) turns on comparison, reward allocation, task interdependence, and identity — Q6 gestures at interdependence and Q7 at identity, but there is no clean item on the *individual attributes/visibility of the standout* as a condition distinct from how reward was handled. Q4 conflates reward-handling with visibility, leaving visibility under-covered as its own condition.
- B005. Leading parentheticals. Several prompts pre-load the answer categories: Q7 "(Ask about a shared sense of team identity, common goals, and inclusive leadership)" hands the participant the theoretical constructs. If the interviewer reads these aloud — and semi-structured guides often do — they become leading. At minimum the guide should flag these as interviewer-only cues, which it does not.
- B006. Recall-anchoring risk. Q2 asks the peer to fix on "a time when a colleague was widely regarded as a standout", and every subsequent item is tethered to that single recalled episode ("in that way", "in that environment", "that period"). If the participant picks a benign example, the whole RQ2 arc yields little. There is no fallback prompt for participants who cannot readily surface a friction-bearing case — a coverage risk, not an ethics one.
- B007. Minor consistency: the guide claims each question "gives balanced room to describe both what eased and what strained teamwork", but Q9 ("give-and-take... sharing expertise or helping one another") is framed almost entirely towards the positive pole; the balancing negative is only in the parenthetical. The stated design principle is not evenly delivered across items.

I concede the opening script, withdrawal rights, and anonymisation are handled well. But "flawless" and "approvable without revision" is a high bar, and B001–B002 alone (double-barrelled items) are the kind of thing a supervisor routinely returns for a rewrite.
